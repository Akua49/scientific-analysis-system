# 3. Literature Review

**Scope.** Five streams that correspond to the five checks of the system: (1) reference rot and link integrity; (2) reference extraction and scholarly metadata; (3) citation accuracy and claim verification; (4) summarisation and abstract–content consistency; (5) text/topic similarity and AI support in academic review. **Totals:** 26 sources — 23 scientific publications (14 from 2020–2025) and 3 technical/normative documents. Foundational works before 2020 are kept where current methods build on them.

## 3.1 Critical review

### Stream 1 — Reference rot (-\> reference check)

Klein et al. \[L1\] analysed over 3.5 million scholarly articles and found that about one in five articles with web references suffers from *reference rot* (links that no longer resolve). Jones et al. \[L2\] showed that even links that still resolve often lead to changed content: about three out of four URI references pointed to content that had drifted from what was originally cited. **Implication:** checking only the HTTP status is insufficient — the system must also compare what the link leads to with the reference entry (title/author/year match), handle redirects, and prefer DOIs (FR-10, FR-11). Baseline in E2 = status-code-only check.

### Stream 2 — Reference parsing and metadata (-\> structure extraction, DOI resolution)

Tkaczyk et al. \[L3\] compared ten open-source reference parsers; GROBID performed best out of the box, and retraining on domain data improved all ML-based parsers. GOST-style Russian references are not represented in their benchmark — a limitation we must test (E1). Crossref \[L4\] is the authoritative registry of DOI metadata; OpenAlex \[L5\] and Semantic Scholar \[L6\] add open abstracts, open-access locations and multilingual coverage. Walters & Wilder \[L7\] found that a large share of bibliographic citations generated by ChatGPT were fabricated (55 % for GPT-3.5, 18 % for GPT-4), and many real ones contained errors — a growing risk for student texts. **Implication:** every reference is resolved against at least two sources; a reference that cannot be found anywhere is flagged as “possibly non-existent” (FR-12).

### Stream 3 — Citation accuracy and verification (-\> citation check)

A systematic review and meta-analysis by Jergas & Baethge \[L8\] estimated that about a quarter of quotations in medical journal articles contain errors, with a substantial share being major errors; Mogull \[L9\] showed that estimates depend strongly on study methodology. Automatic verification is studied as *scientific claim verification*: SciFact \[L10\] retrieves rationale sentences from abstracts and labels claims as SUPPORTS / REFUTES. Semantic matching relies on sentence embeddings: Sentence-BERT \[L11\], its multilingual distillation \[L12\], LaBSE \[L13\] and multilingual E5 \[L14\] allow cross-lingual comparison (RU/KZ citation vs. EN source). Limitations: source full text is often unavailable; Kazakh is under-represented in training data. **Implication:** two-stage approach — embedding retrieval of the most similar source fragment, then entailment (NLI) classification; labels *supported / partially supported / not supported / source unavailable*; TF-IDF cosine as the baseline (E3).

### Stream 4 — Summarisation and abstract consistency (-\> abstract check)

Long scientific documents require discourse-aware or long-context models \[L15, L16\]. Lexical metrics such as ROUGE \[L17\] correlate poorly with meaning; BERTScore \[L18\] compares texts in embedding space. For *consistency* between a summary and its source, NLI-based SummaC \[L19\] and AlignScore \[L20\] detect unsupported statements. **Implication:** the system (a) generates a reference summary of the body, (b) measures coverage of the body’s key points by the author’s abstract, and (c) flags abstract sentences not supported by the body; validated against expert ratings (E4).

### Stream 5 — Similarity, topics and AI-assisted review (-\> topic check, requirements check)

Foltýnek et al. \[L21\] reviewed academic plagiarism detection and distinguish lexical, syntactic and semantic methods; semantic (embedding-based) methods detect paraphrased ideas that lexical methods miss — the same applies to topic similarity across years. Liang et al. \[L22\] found LLM feedback on papers overlaps with human reviewers at a level comparable to human–human overlap, but is less specific; Kousha & Thelwall \[L23\] conclude AI suits supporting tasks such as screening and format checks. **Implication:** requirement checks are rule-based where measurable and LLM-assisted (with quoted evidence) only for qualitative items; all results are advisory.

### Gap

Plagiarism tools detect copied text; citation tools check formatting or retractions. No reviewed tool verifies, in one workflow, link validity, citation content, abstract consistency, custom requirements and topic repetition — and none targets Russian/Kazakh student theses.
